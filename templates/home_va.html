Know what is due. Know what it costs to be late.

How it works

One working day, from the first circular to the last alert.

Five moments from an ordinary day in late September: four at a listed company, one in a bank's risk team. The companies, people and headlines are samples. The rules, forms and time limits are the ones Delta Filings applies.

  1. 09:00Regulatory news
  2. 10:30The calendar and late fees
  3. 13:00Board papers and drafting
  4. 16:00Insider-trading record
  5. 18:10Counterparty risk
09:00 01 · News

The morning's circulars, already sorted by subject.

Delta Filings reads the SEBI and RBI feeds through the day and tags every circular, order, consultation and press release by what it is about.

You open on the subjects that fit your job. A listed company's compliance officer sees LODR and insider trading first. A risk officer sees lending, NBFC rules, prudential norms and enforcement.

SEBI orders are also matched against a risk team's counterparties. That comes back at 18:10.

10:30 02 · Calendar

The AGM is tomorrow. AOC-4 is due 30 days after it.

The calendar is built from the company's profile: its type, whether it is listed, and whether it has GST registration, employees, foreign investment or TDS to deduct. Change the profile and the calendar follows.

Every line says what lateness costs and how the figure was reached. AOC-4 and MGT-7 each run at ₹100 a day with no upper limit, so two months late on both comes to ₹12,000.

Events add their own filings. Log a director's appointment and DIR-12 is due in 30 days. Allot shares on a private placement and PAS-3 is due in 15. Each DIN carries its own DIR-3 KYC date on the three-year cycle, and DSC expiry sits on the same calendar, because an expired certificate blocks every form that director signs.

13:00 03 · Meetings and drafting

The board meets. Its follow-up filings are dated the same afternoon.

The agenda was picked last week from a library of standard items, such as appointing an additional director under s.161 and approving borrowings under s.179(3)(d). Delta drafted the notice, agenda, resolutions and minutes skeleton as one Word file, and checked the notice period: seven days for a board meeting, 21 clear days for an AGM.

Marking the meeting held puts the follow-ups on the calendar with their dates: the Regulation 30 disclosure, DIR-12, MGT-14 for the borrowing resolution, and minutes to be signed within 30 days.

Then the exchange letter. Draft writes it with AI in the company's own style, using its past NSE filings as the reference. Nothing is submitted for you, and every draft carries a notice to review it first.

16:00 04 · Insider trading

The quarter's first estimates go to the auditor, into a record nobody can quietly edit.

Sharing unpublished price-sensitive information is logged in the structured digital database that SEBI's insider-trading regulations require. Entries can be added and never edited or deleted.

Each entry stores a SHA-256 hash of the previous entry's hash plus its own contents. One altered row breaks the chain, and the check names the first row that fails. A mistake is fixed with a correction entry that points at the original.

The quarter ends tomorrow. The trading window closes on 1 October and reopens 48 hours after results. While it is closed, pre-clearance cannot be approved, and approvals last seven trading days.

18:10 05 · Risk desk

In a bank's risk team, a borrower shows up in an IBBI announcement.

The risk team lists every company the bank is exposed to, with the rupee amount: borrowers, vendors, investees and guarantors. Delta checks each one against exchange filings, SEBI orders, IBBI insolvency announcements and one-day share-price falls of 5% or more. IBBI covers unlisted companies too.

Each signal carries points that fade with age. They add up to a score out of 100, with a band and the reasons written beside it. Critical and high signals go to Telegram.

The director network shows who sits on the boards of more than one counterparty. The committee report is ready for the next risk committee meeting.

Sources and limits

Where the data comes from, and where it stops.

A compliance tool that overstates itself is worse than none. This is what Delta reads, and what you should still check yourself.

Sources

SEBI
Circulars, orders, recovery proceedings and press releases from SEBI's own feed. Its orders are also matched against risk-desk counterparties.
RBI
Notifications and press releases, tagged by subject: NBFC, KYC and AML, lending, payments, FEMA and more.
IBBI
Public announcements of insolvency (CIRP) and liquidation. These cover unlisted companies as well as listed ones.
NSE
Corporate announcements, governance filings with board composition and DINs, and share prices.
Rulebook
Written from the Acts, rules and regulations, with each rule linked to its source, usually the regulator's own site.

What to check yourself

  • Late fees are estimates. The real figure can depend on share capital, turnover or the tax involved, so each one shows its basis and says to verify at source.
  • MCA updates are not in the news feed, because the MCA site blocks automated access. Delta does not file forms on the MCA portal for you.
  • Unlisted companies' MCA records such as charges, filing defaults and struck-off status need a licensed data source. The risk desk does not scrape them.
  • Drafts are drafts. Nothing goes to an exchange or a regulator unless you send it.
  • Name matches can be wrong. When a SEBI order or IBBI notice matches a counterparty only in part, the signal says so and asks you to verify.
